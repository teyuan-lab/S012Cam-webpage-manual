What is polarization? Start with the electric field

In free space, light is a transverse electromagnetic wave. Its electric field E and magnetic field B oscillate perpendicular to the direction of travel and to each other. We normally describe optical polarization by the behaviour of E. A vertical E does not mean the light travels upward.

Compare the travelling-wave snapshot in Fig. 1(a) with the field-tip paths in Fig. 1(b–d).

Linear: E oscillates along a fixed axis. Circular: its tip rotates at constant length. Elliptical: its tip traces an ellipse. Two perpendicular E components and their relative phase determine the state. Linear polarization is an axis: 0° and 180° are equivalent.

A basic property of light, distinct from colour

Brightness, wavelength and polarization describe different aspects of light. Wavelength determines spectral colour; polarization describes E. Two red beams can have perpendicular linear polarizations. A mixed beam can also contain red, green and blue components with different polarization states.

Fig. 2(a–c) gives three illustrative wavelength bands with independent polarization axes.

Reflection, absorption and phase delay can depend on wavelength, so polarization after an optical material can vary across the spectrum. Compare S012Cam’s R, G and B channels. They are broad sensor bands, not three exact wavelengths.

What is unpolarized light?

Unpolarized light still has an electric field. Its transverse components fluctuate so that, over the observation time, no polarization state is preferred. Ordinary thermal light is often approximately unpolarized before reflection or scattering. A fixed circularly polarized wave is different: its field rotates in a definite, ordered way.

The time samples in Fig. 3(a–c) distinguish unpolarized, partially polarized and linearly polarized light.

Partially polarized light lies between fully polarized and unpolarized light. DoLP describes only the linear part. DoLP = 0 does not prove that light is unpolarized: purely circular polarization also gives no modulation through a rotating ideal linear analyzer. The four-angle S012Cam method does not measure the circular Stokes component S₃.

How does Fresnel reflection polarize light?

At a boundary between media, the Fresnel equations give different reflection coefficients for two E orientations. The plane of incidence contains the incoming ray and the surface normal. s means E perpendicular to that plane; p means E parallel to it. Reflection can therefore change the balance of s and p and turn unpolarized incident light into partially polarized reflected light.

Use the normal and incidence plane in Fig. 4 to define the s and p components.

The curves in Fig. 5 show why the reflected p component vanishes at the ideal Brewster angle.

For transparent, nonabsorbing dielectrics, the Brewster angle obeys tan θB = n₂/n₁. At this angle Rp = 0, so reflection of unpolarized incident light is ideally fully s-polarized. Air-to-water gives about 53° and air-to-common-glass about 56° from the normal. Real rough surfaces, coatings, background light and multiple reflections reduce the purity.

For a horizontal table or lake, the incidence plane is vertical, so the strongly reflected s component has horizontal E. This is why a vertically transmitting polarizer can suppress this glare. At normal incidence s and p have equal reflectance for an isotropic surface. Metal reflection needs a different, absorbing-medium treatment; the simple dielectric Brewster rule does not apply.

Experiment: a glass table or water surface

Use your eyes. Look obliquely at a bright reflection on a horizontal glass table or calm water. Hold a linear polarizer in front of one eye and rotate it slowly without changing your viewing direction. Watch the same reflected patch become bright, dark and bright again. The aim is to see that reflection favours one E direction. No camera is needed.

How does Rayleigh scattering polarize light?

When light encounters molecules or particles much smaller than its wavelength, its electric field drives oscillating dipoles. A dipole radiates strongly perpendicular to its oscillation and not along that axis. Looking from the side selects one transverse E component, producing polarized scattered light.

The person at the centre of the dome in Fig. 7 is the observer; each sky direction is viewed from that point.

The side-view geometry in Fig. 8 shows how a 90° scattering direction selects the transverse electric field.

The ideal curve in Fig. 9 reaches maximum DoLP at 90°.

The ideal model is most strongly polarized at a 90° scattering angle. The scattered intensity has a roughly 1/λ⁴ dependence in the Rayleigh regime, making shorter visible wavelengths scatter more strongly. Blue colour and polarization arise from related scattering physics, but they are different observables.

What is a Polaroid polarizer?

Fig. 10 shows Edwin H. Land, whose work made practical sheet polarizers possible.

Before instant photography: the sheet polarizer

In the late 1920s, Edwin H. Land worked on an inexpensive synthetic sheet polarizer. Rather than growing one large crystal, early sheets embedded many tiny aligned dichroic crystals in a transparent material. He filed the application behind US1,918,848 in 1929; the patent was granted in 1933.

Polaroid Corporation was established in 1937. Polarizing sheets enabled practical glare-reducing sunglasses, camera filters and stereoscopic viewing, before the company became famous for instant photography. Later molecular sheet designs, including iodine–PVA films, use aligned absorbing species rather than the early microcrystal suspension.

Harvard Baker Library: Invention of the Polarizer · US1,918,848 · Rowland Institute: Edwin H. Land

A Polaroid-type sheet polarizer is an absorbing, dichroic linear polarizer. In a common iodine–PVA design, stretching aligns polymer chains and absorbing species. One E component is strongly absorbed and the orthogonal component passes more readily. The transmission axis is the direction of E that passes, not the absorbing-chain direction. Not every polarizer uses this material; wire-grid and crystal polarizers work differently.

Follow the red absorbed y component and blue transmitted x component in Fig. 11; both are perpendicular to propagation z.

The angle dependence in Fig. 12 predicts the bright and dark orientations seen through a rotating sheet.

An ideal linear polarizer passes half the intensity of unpolarized input. For linearly polarized input, parallel axes give maximum transmission and crossed axes give extinction. Real sheets have absorption and finite extinction. A photographic CPL adds a retardation layer to a linear polarizer; for S012Cam, use the specified linear polarizer.

Place two linear polarizers in front of a diffuse light source. Rotate one through 180°. You should see two bright orientations separated by a dark orientation. Two sheets establish their relative axes, not an absolute vertical reference.

Find a polarizer’s transmission axis using reflection

Use the known polarization direction of glare as your reference. On a horizontal glass or water surface, glare near the Brewster geometry is predominantly horizontal. When it becomes darkest through your polarizer, the transmission axis is approximately vertical.

First use the viewing geometry in Fig. 13(a), then rotate the sheet to minimum glare as in Fig. 13(b).

  1. Find a flat horizontal glass table or water surface with a clear reflection. Avoid a strongly polarized source as the reference.
  2. View at about 34–37° above the surface: roughly 53–56° from its normal. Adjust the angle for the strongest brightness change.
  3. Rotate the sheet around your viewing direction, keeping it roughly facing you. Stop at minimum reflected brightness.
  4. Without changing its orientation, draw a vertical line on the protective film near a corner. This marks the transmission axis.
  5. For the Rotator, set position 1 and align the mark with the double-headed axis arrows. Remove the protective film after fitting.

Keep that orientation while making the transmission-axis mark shown in Fig. 14.

Check before measuring

Keep the landscape iPhone level and measure the same horizontal glare. Its dominant E should be horizontal relative to the phone. A vertical result suggests a 90° mounting error; check the filter and axis reference. This is a practical orientation check, not a complete calibration of the measurement system.

Polarization in the blue sky: where should you look?

Sunlight scattered by air molecules forms a patterned polarization field across the sky. Under clear conditions, strong linear polarization is often found about 90° from the Sun. That is an angular distance from the Sun, not a fixed elevation above the horizon. In the simple single-scattering model, E is perpendicular to the plane containing the Sun, scattering molecule and observer.

The right-angle construction in Fig. 15 helps locate sky about 90° from the Sun; compare it with the full dome in Fig. 7.

Clouds, haze, aerosols, multiple scattering and ground reflections modify the ideal pattern and often lower DoLP. Through a wide-angle view, different parts of the sky can dim by different amounts when a polarizer rotates.

Experiment: map three sky directions

Keep the camera away from the Sun. On a clear day, compare three patches at different angular distances from it, including about 90°. Use the same settings and compare R, G and B DoLP maps. Record clouds and haze with the result; do not expect the ideal value of 1.

Polarization from a computer LCD

A typical LCD uses a backlight, polarizers and an electrically controlled liquid-crystal layer. The liquid crystal changes the polarization transformation; an analyzer converts that change into brightness. Light leaving many LCDs is therefore strongly linearly polarized. Its axis depends on the panel, its orientation, wavelength and viewing angle.

Compare the normally-white TN example in Fig. 16(a), with voltage off, and Fig. 16(b), with voltage on. The molecular directors and optical electric field are different quantities.

The point measurement in Fig. 17 demonstrates strong linear polarization from this LCD.

A photograph of the sky displayed on an LCD is not a measurement of sky polarization. The screen’s own optics set the emitted polarization. Other display technologies, covers and compensation films may produce different, including elliptical, states; do not assume every screen is an ideal linear source.

Experiment: rotate a sheet in front of a white screen

Show a plain white field on an LCD. Look through a linear polarizer with one eye and rotate it without changing your viewing direction. Find the brightest and darkest orientations. Then show red, green and blue fields and repeat. Record actual observations rather than assigning a universal screen axis.

The coloured patterns in Fig. 18 illustrate stress birefringence between crossed polarizers.

Experiment: transparent plastic in front of an LCD

Place a clear plastic spoon or ruler between the LCD and the rotating analyzer. Stress birefringence gives different polarization components different phase delays; their wavelength dependence can produce coloured patterns. Rotate the object or gently flex a suitable plastic sample. The colours are not a direct stress scale without a calibrated material model.

From the lesson to a S012Cam measurement

A rotating linear analyzer samples intensity at four axes. For ideal, stable, linear-intensity data, the four frames estimate S₀, S₁ and S₂. The display colour encodes AoLP; it is not the original spectral colour. When DoLP is close to zero, the angle is poorly defined.

Use the key in Fig. 19 to interpret the encoded AoLP and DoLP of a S012Cam result.

Four-angle formulae and their assumptions
S₀ ≈ [(I₀ + I₉₀) + (I₄₅ + I₁₃₅)] / 2
S₁ = I₀ − I₉₀
S₂ = I₄₅ − I₁₃₅
DoLP = √(S₁² + S₂²) / S₀
AoLP = ½ atan2(S₂, S₁) mod 180°

Ideally both orthogonal sums equal S₀. This assumes calibrated analyzer angles, stable lighting, unsaturated linear data and the same scene point in every image. Noise, weak channels and movement can bias the result. Four linear-analyzer frames do not determine S₃.
